When I started my master’s degree, I expected to become a better wildlife researcher. I didn’t realize how much I would grow outside of research.

Just a month ago, I successfully defended my master’s thesis, “The Effect of Phenotypic Divergence and Environmental Conditions on the Reproductive Life History of Squamate Reptiles.”

Reaching this point took a lot of work. Graduate school taught me how to develop and conduct research, work through uncertainty, and communicate science. It also tested me personally, especially during my final year when I suffered a torn ACL and underwent two knee surgeries.

At the same time, my master’s gave me experiences I’ll carry with me far beyond my thesis.

I spent summers traveling across California and the Channel Islands, capturing snakes and lizards for fieldwork. I worked with Penn State’s College of Agricultural Sciences Office of Access and Equity, providing tutoring and mentorship to underrepresented students. I also served as a group leader with the Student Diplomacy Corps, helping lead high school students to Southeast Alaska to learn about the region’s ecology and Indigenous cultures.

Looking back, my master’s degree was about much more than completing a research project. It helped me become a better scientist, mentor, leader, and person.

I’m especially grateful to my advisors, Dr. David Miller and Dr. Amanda Sparkman, for their guidance and support throughout the process.

And I’m excited to keep going.

I’m now continuing my education as a Ph.D. student in Ecology at Penn State University. I’m looking forward to the next chapter and all the lessons and experiences that come with it.
